# Sources of Long-Run Equity Wealth — A Grounded Literature Map of the Return Engine

**Scope.** This maps the *absolute wealth-creation / compounding* layer of equity returns — the "return engine" — as distinct from the cross-sectional relative-outperformance (factor) layer, which is mapped separately. Every school is framed as a **mechanism** (the economic "why"), never as a product, and scored on three deliberately-separated durability questions plus market-evolution class, forward evidence, OOS/international survival, falsifiers, a verdict, and an **expression-level** tag.

**The organizing question.** Which forces are tied to persistent economic/behavioral constraints likely to remain relevant over 20–30+ years, versus historical patterns that may not recur — and at what level (market / factor / sector / firm) does each genuinely express?

**How to read the three durability questions (kept strictly distinct):**
- **Q1 — Does the mechanism EXIST?** (empirical/theoretical reality of the effect)
- **Q2 — WHY should it survive being KNOWN?** (a paid risk or a structural friction = durable; "not yet arbitraged" = fragile)
- **Q3 — Does the CURRENT way of CAPTURING it still work** after costs, crowding, and market adaptation? (a mechanism can persist while a crowded implementation of it decays)

**Discipline applied throughout.** A mechanism→sector link must be *derived from the mechanism*, never inferred from "the sector recently outperformed." Recent-winner patterns are flagged as such. Weak/contested evidence is called out explicitly rather than smoothed over.

**Grounding note.** Claims are anchored to named primary sources verified via search (journal pages, NBER, SSRN, Brookings, Princeton UP, UBS/DMS Yearbook, Damodaran/NYU Stern, IESE/Fernandez). Two citation corrections surfaced during grounding: Arnott & Bernstein "What Risk Premium Is *Normal*?" is **FAJ 2002 58(2)** (the 2003 FAJ paper is Bernstein & Arnott "Earnings Growth: The Two Percent Dilution"). One magnitude set (Pastor-Stambaugh variance ratios) is from abstract/secondary summaries, not a line-by-line table read — flagged in place.

# PART I — THE EQUITY RISK PREMIUM & ITS PUZZLES

**Cross-cutting frame.** These five schools are *not* five different edges. They are **one phenomenon** — the market-wide equity premium, the single most robust source of long-run equity wealth — plus four competing *explanations* for why its price of risk is so high, plus a magnitude debate. All the explanation-schools agree on the load-bearing point for a mechanism gate: **the ERP is a paid, undiversifiable risk premium, not an anomaly — it survives being known by construction.** They differ on the deep structural "why," and those structural claims are largely *observationally equivalent* and hard to distinguish. The one genuinely contested empirical question is **magnitude** (School 5).

## 1. Mehra–Prescott Equity Premium Puzzle (1985)

- **(1) Core claim.** The historical US equity premium (~6% real) is an order of magnitude too large to be explained by the standard consumption-CAPM under any *plausible* risk aversion — hence a "puzzle."
- **(2) Mechanism.** Consumption-CAPM: premium = γ × cov(equity returns, consumption growth). Aggregate consumption is very smooth (~1–2% vol) and weakly covaries with stocks, so matching 6% needs γ ≈ 20–40 vs a plausible 1–5. Weil (1989) added the joint **risk-free-rate puzzle**: high γ also implies a counterfactually high risk-free rate.
- **(Q1) Exists?** Yes — the *premium* is robust (Fama-French 2002: 7.43% realized 1951–2000); the "puzzle" is its unexplained *magnitude* under CRRA, one of the most-replicated facts in finance.
- **(Q2) Survives being known?** Yes, cleanly — compensation for undiversifiable aggregate risk; someone must hold the market's equity risk and is paid to. Uncrowdable by construction.
- **(Q3) Current capture.** The cleanest case in the whole landscape: hold the cap-weighted index — single-digit bps, uncrowdable (it *is* the market), no timing skill. The only live debate is whether the *forward* premium is as high as the historical 6% (see School 5).
- **(4) Classification.** **PERSISTENT economic force** (existence/sign); *level* is condition-dependent.
- **(5) 20–30yr evidence.** Existence near-certain; magnitude likely lower than 6% — ex-ante estimates (Fama-French 2.55–4.32%) sit well below realized because part of the realized number was one-time valuation re-rating.
- **(6) OOS / international.** Genuinely contested on magnitude. *Skeptic camp:* Jorion & Goetzmann (1999) — the US had the highest real return of 39 markets (4.3% vs 0.8% median); survivorship may explain ~⅓. *Robust camp:* DMS find a positive premium in every continuous-history market. Net: existence internationally robust; the 6% US number is partly US-specific.
- **(7) Falsifiers.** A multi-decade major-market window where diversified equities fail to beat bonds (never observed over 30yr). The *puzzle* (not the premium) dissolves if any resolution model (Schools 2–4) is validated — Kocherlakota (1996) concluded none fully does.
- **(8) Verdict.** **Agree** — robust premium *and* still-open puzzle. The strongest single source of long-run equity wealth and the null every other school must beat. Caveats: 6% overstates the *forward* premium; the theoretical puzzle remains unresolved after 40 years.
- **Expression level.** **Market-wide** — premium of the whole market over the risk-free asset, priced by covariance with *aggregate* consumption. Explicitly not style/sector/firm.
- **(9) Citations.** Mehra & Prescott (1985) "The Equity Premium: A Puzzle," *JME* 15(2) · Weil (1989) *JME* 24(3) · Kocherlakota (1996) "It's Still a Puzzle," *JEL* 34(1) · Fama & French (2002) "The Equity Premium," *JF* 57(2) · Jorion & Goetzmann (1999) "Global Stock Markets in the Twentieth Century," *JF* 54(3).

## 2. Bansal–Yaron Long-Run Risk (2004)

- **(1) Core claim.** The premium is compensation for aversion to *small, highly persistent shocks to expected long-run consumption growth and to time-varying uncertainty* — not to short-run consumption volatility.
- **(2) Mechanism.** Three required ingredients: (a) a small persistent predictable component in consumption growth (AR(1) ≈ 0.98 monthly); (b) stochastic volatility of consumption; (c) Epstein-Zin-Weil recursive preferences with γ ≈ 10 and EIS ψ > 1, which decouples risk aversion from intertemporal substitution so the agent prices long-horizon (continuation-utility) shocks separately.
- **(Q1) Exists? — CONTESTED, the central vulnerability.** The persistent component sits at the edge of statistical detectability in ~90yr of consumption data (weak identification). *For:* Ortu-Tamoni-Tebaldi (2013), Liu-Matthies (2022). *Against:* Constantinides-Ghosh (2011) find implied predictability far exceeds data; Maio (2024) disputes the Liu-Matthies pricing result.
- **(Q2) Survives being known?** Yes — a rational risk-compensation story (investors paid to hold assets that lose value when long-run growth dims). Not an arbitrage. The school's strongest feature.
- **(Q3) Current capture.** Capture is trivial (it *is* market beta). But the *specific calibration* is contested: Beeler-Campbell (2012) and Constantinides-Ghosh show it over-predicts return/consumption predictability; the BKY (2012) re-calibration then over-predicts return-*volatility* forecastability.
- **(4) Classification.** **PERSISTENT force** if real — but it is a *model of why the premium exists*, not a tradable edge.
- **(5) 20–30yr evidence.** Direction durable (growth and macro uncertainty are permanently stochastic); calibrated *magnitude* not reliably forecastable.
- **(6) OOS / critiques.** Beeler-Campbell (2012, canonical); Constantinides-Ghosh (2011); disaster bridge via Nakamura-Steinsson-Barro-Ursúa; live pricing dispute Liu-Matthies (2022) vs Maio (2024).
- **(7) Falsifiers.** i.i.d. consumption growth (kills it — and nearly what low-power data show); true EIS < 1 (flips the signs — and macro EIS estimates are often below 1); Epstein-Farhi-Strzalecki (2014) show the required early-resolution preference implies implausible willingness-to-pay (~25%+ of consumption).
- **(8) Verdict.** **Split — lean agree on concept, genuinely weak on the quantitative structural claim.** The *concept* (equities load on feared persistent growth/uncertainty shocks) is a legitimate paid-risk mechanism; the *BY/BKY calibration as THE explanation* is weakly and contestedly evidenced. Takeaway: a reason to hold broad equity, not a factor edge or a number to lever against.
- **Expression level.** Primarily **market-wide**; reaches **style/factor** via cash-flow duration — Bansal-Dittmar-Lundblad (2005) show cash-flow consumption betas explain >60% of the value/size/momentum cross-section. Not sector/firm.
- **(9) Citations.** Bansal & Yaron (2004) "Risks for the Long Run," *JF* 59(4) · Beeler & Campbell (2012) *Critical Finance Review* 1(1) · Constantinides & Ghosh (2011) *Review of Asset Pricing Studies* 1(1) · Bansal, Dittmar & Lundblad (2005) *JF* 60(4) · Bansal, Kiku & Yaron (2012) *Critical Finance Review* 1(1).

## 3. Rare Disasters (Rietz 1988 → Barro 2006 → Gabaix / Wachter / Barro-Ursúa)

- **(1) Core claim.** The premium is not a puzzle but fair compensation for a small, ever-present probability of rare macroeconomic catastrophes that happen not to have struck most peaceful post-war samples.
- **(2) Mechanism.** Add a low-probability, large-magnitude jump-down in aggregate consumption to a Lucas-tree economy. Marginal utility spikes when the disaster hits, so equities are genuinely risky and a *moderate* γ ≈ 3.5–4 reproduces the ~7% levered premium and the low risk-free rate. Textbook **peso problem**: a US-only sample under-samples the tail.
- **(Q1) Exists?** Yes — disasters are documented. Barro-Ursúa (2008) assembled long-run series for 24–36 countries: ~95 consumption / 152 GDP crises (≥10% decline), implying ~3.5%/yr disaster probability, mean size ~21–22%.
- **(Q2) Survives being known?** Yes, cleanly — uninsurable *systematic* risk (wars, depressions, pandemics are aggregate). No counterparty in aggregate; investors are structurally paid to warehouse catastrophe risk. Passes the mechanism gate strongly.
- **(Q3) Current capture.** Two routes: (a) hold equities — no crowding problem, but you bear the actual disaster; (b) *sell* disaster insurance (short OTM puts / variance) — harvests a real variance/skew premium but is fragile and blows up in the disaster state (2008, 2020, Volmageddon). A Chicago Fed (2025) note documents a secular decline in the variance risk premium consistent with crowding. Variable-disaster models (Gabaix 2012, Wachter 2013) make the premium largest exactly when disaster risk is perceived high — hardest to hold.
- **(4) Classification.** **PERSISTENT economic force**, with a condition-dependent *measurement* wrinkle (whether a given window contains a disaster is path-dependent).
- **(5) 20–30yr evidence.** Disasters kept arriving after the datasets were built (2008, COVID-2020) — direct OOS confirmation the tail is live. But realized 20–30yr return is path-dependent: you can collect the premium for decades and surrender much of it in one event (that is the deal).
- **(6) OOS / international + calibration debate.** The Barro-Ursúa multi-country dataset *is* the OOS evidence. Central critique: **Julliard & Ghosh (2012)** — using GMM/empirical likelihood that *allows* state probabilities to differ from sample frequencies, the consumption-CCAPM is still rejected; observed disasters are too small unless they recur every 6–10 years. Nakamura-Steinsson-Barro-Ursúa (2013) add partial recovery (~half the drop reverses) → substantially *smaller* premium.
- **(7) Falsifiers.** Required disaster frequency/severity far exceeding the historical record (Julliard-Ghosh argue the strong form is already falsified); largely transitory rather than permanent disasters (Nakamura et al.). The peso problem cuts both ways — near-unfalsifiability is itself the epistemic weakness.
- **(8) Verdict.** **Agree on mechanism, genuinely weak/contested on magnitude.** One of the most mechanism-sound stories (uninsurable systematic risk, survives publication by construction), but inherently hard to test and the strong "disasters *alone* explain the premium" claim is probably overstated. Best read: a real, important *component*, not a complete standalone solution.
- **Expression level.** Primarily **market-wide**, with a genuine cross-sectional/duration nuance: assets differ in disaster exposure. Farhi-Gabaix (2016) price disaster exposure in **currencies** (carry); high-beta, cyclical, high-operating-leverage, long-duration **sectors/firms** are more disaster-exposed.
- **(9) Citations.** Rietz (1988) *JME* 22(1) · Barro (2006) "Rare Disasters and Asset Markets…," *QJE* 121(3) · Barro & Ursúa (2008) "Macroeconomic Crises since 1870," *BPEA* 2008(1) · Gabaix (2012) "Variable Rare Disasters," *QJE* 127(2); Wachter (2013) *JF* 68(3) · Julliard & Ghosh (2012) "Can Rare Events Explain the Equity Premium Puzzle?" *RFS* 25(10) (the critique).

## 4. Campbell–Cochrane Habit Formation (1999)

- **(1) Core claim.** An agent whose utility depends on consumption *relative to a slow-moving external habit* has countercyclical risk aversion (high in recessions), which reproduces the large, volatile, countercyclical, predictable premium plus a low stable risk-free rate.
- **(2) Mechanism.** Utility `(C−X)^(1−γ)/(1−γ)` with external habit `X`; state variable = surplus consumption ratio `S=(C−X)/C`. Local risk aversion = `γ/S_t`, **countercyclical**: recessions → S low → risk aversion spikes → high price of risk → high expected returns. Delivers P/D-ratio return predictability without predicting dividend growth — a key stylized fact.
- **(Q1) Exists?** Partially/indirectly. The *reduced-form symptom* — a countercyclical, highly volatile conditional Sharpe ratio — is well documented (Lettau-Ludvigson `cay`). The *deep structural object* (`γ/S_t`, latent `S_t`) is inferred, not measured.
- **(Q2) Survives being known?** Yes — a *preference structure*, not an arbitrage. Equities lose money in recessions when marginal utility is highest; knowing this does not make recessions pleasant.
- **(Q3) Current capture.** Static exposure (hold equities) — durable. *Timing* on the countercyclical signal — contested: predictability is real in-sample/internationally but `cay`-style predictors weakened OOS post-publication. **Directly relevant to this project's repeated finding that single-series market timing loses to reactive vol-management:** habit explains *why* a countercyclical signal exists but does not guarantee it beats a cheap reactive baseline net of costs.
- **(4) Classification.** **PERSISTENT force** for the premium; the *timing signal* is closer to condition-dependent (predictability decays). Persistent premium, fragile timing.
- **(5) 20–30yr evidence.** Premium continuation rests on the durable fact that equities do badly in bad times. Caveats: structural habit not directly identified; constant-rf calibration fragile; Ljungqvist-Uhlig welfare pathologies cast doubt on the literal preference.
- **(6) OOS / international + critiques.** Engsted-Hyde-Møller (2010) — surplus ratio captures time-varying expected stock and bond returns for most countries, but with "economically significant pricing errors." Cross-section weakness: built for the aggregate market, struggles with value/size. **Ljungqvist-Uhlig (2015)**: the preferences imply bizarre welfare conclusions — attacks structural realism, not the reduced-form fit.
- **(7) Falsifiers.** Acyclical/procyclical price of risk in long samples; no OOS forecasting power for the surplus ratio; the literal preference (arguably partly falsified by Ljungqvist-Uhlig); being dominated/made redundant by LRR or disaster models on the same moments.
- **(8) Verdict.** **Agree on the reduced form; genuinely weak/underidentified on the deep structure.** The countercyclical time-varying price of risk is real and corroborated, but habit is one of several *observationally-similar* preference models (habit vs LRR vs disasters fit the same aggregate moments), and taken literally the specification is damaged by welfare pathologies. Strong as a reason the premium exists and is countercyclical; weak as a uniquely identified truth or a tradable timing edge net of costs.
- **Expression level.** Primarily **market-wide** (single-state aggregate model; motivates a market-wide countercyclical timing variable, not a cross-sectional rank). Value-premium extensions push toward **style/factor**, but that is where the base model is weakest. Not sector/firm.
- **(9) Citations.** Campbell & Cochrane (1999) "By Force of Habit," *JPE* 107(2) · Cochrane (2016) "The Habit Habit," Hoover WP 16105 · Ljungqvist & Uhlig (2015) "Comment on the Campbell–Cochrane Habit Model," *JPE* 123(5) · Lettau & Ludvigson (2001) *JF* (countercyclical Sharpe / `cay`) · Engsted, Hyde & Møller (2010) *J. Int'l Money & Finance* 29(7).

## 5. ERP Magnitude & the Forward-Looking Debate (Dimson-Marsh-Staunton / Fernandez / Damodaran)

- **(1) Core claim.** The realized long-run premium is robustly positive (world equities ~5.2% real p.a. 1900–2024, beating bonds/bills/inflation in all 21 continuous-history markets), but the realized number **overstates the forward premium** because of survivorship/success bias and one-time valuation re-rating — DMS put the forward world premium at only ~3–3.5% geometric.
- **(2) Mechanism.** *Why it exists* — equities are a residual claim; holders bear undiversifiable systematic risk; ERP is market-clearing compensation. *Why realized > forward* — three wedges: (a) cross-market survivorship (histories anchored on winners like the US; DMS add Russia and China, both →−100%); (b) one-time valuation re-rating (much of the 20th-c. US return came from rising multiples / falling required returns); (c) unanticipated good luck.
- **(Q1) Exists?** Yes — robustly. DMS now cover 35 markets (21 continuous); equities beat bonds, bills, and inflation in all 21. About as well-measured as anything in empirical finance.
- **(Q2) Survives being known / is magnitude decaying?** Survives (compensation, not mispricing). But magnitude is genuinely lower forward: market integration lowers required premia; elevated starting valuations compress implied premia.
- **(Q3) Current capture / forward estimate.** Hold the broad market. Forward estimates converge *below* realized: **Damodaran implied ERP 4.23% (start-2026)**; **DMS forward ~3–3.5% geometric**; **Fernandez survey US MRP 5.5%** (what practitioners *say they use* — structurally higher than model-implied). That ~3.5% / 4.2% / 5.5% spread *is* the historical-vs-implied-vs-survey gap.
- **(4) Classification.** **Split — PERSISTENT** that an ERP exists and is positive; **CONDITION-DEPENDENT** for the specific realized US ~6% (a partly-lucky draw).
- **(5) 20–30yr evidence.** Existence strong (125yr, 35 markets, every continuous market positive). Magnitude at ~6% unlikely — DMS's own decomposition attributes much to non-repeatable re-rating; higher valuations mechanically imply lower forward returns.
- **(6) OOS / international.** The DMS 21–35 country panel *is* the key OOS evidence, designed to defeat US survivorship. Russia (−100%, 1917) and China (−100%, 1949) are the "non-success" markets US-only histories silently drop; including them lowers the global average — the empirical core of the overstatement argument.
- **(7) Falsifiers.** Existence falsified if a survivorship-corrected multi-decade panel showed equities failing to beat bills (not observed). Magnitude claim falsified if realized forward premia matched historical US ~6% *without* a valuation tailwind. Disappearance conditions: total market destruction (Russia/China tail) or a permanent negative-premium regime (historically transient).
- **(8) Verdict.** Positive ERP exists, robustly measured across 20+ countries — **Agree (strong).** Survives being known as a paid systematic risk — **Agree (strong).** Realized US ~6% overstates the forward premium — **Agree (well-evidenced; DMS core thesis).** Best forward estimate materially below 6% (~3.5% DMS geo to ~4.2% Damodaran implied) — **Agree, but the exact number is model-dependent** (survey practice sits higher at 5.5%).
- **Expression level.** Essentially **market-wide / country-level**. Main sub-structure is the **cross-country** dimension (wide dispersion in national premia) — the axis the survivorship argument operates on. Not a cross-sectional security edge.
- **(9) Citations.** Dimson, Marsh & Staunton (2002) *Triumph of the Optimists*, Princeton UP · DMS *UBS Global Investment Returns Yearbook 2025* (125yr, 35 markets; world real equities 5.2%) · DMS "The Worldwide Equity Premium: A Smaller Puzzle" (SSRN 891620) · Fernandez et al. "Survey: MRP and RF used for 54 countries in 2025" (IESE/SSRN; US MRP 5.5%) · Damodaran "Equity Risk Premiums — 2026 Edition" (NYU Stern; implied ERP 4.23%).

# PART II — RETURN DECOMPOSITION & THE GROWTH-vs-RETURNS LITERATURE

## 6. Grinold-Kroner / Gordon Return Decomposition

- **(1) Core claim.** Long-run expected return = income yield (dividend + net buyback) + nominal earnings growth (inflation + real growth − dilution) ± Δvaluation; strip the non-repeatable repricing term and the durable forward return is essentially income yield plus modest real growth — materially below the naïve historical average.
- **(2) Mechanism.** Two layers: an **accounting identity** (Gordon: R = D/P + g, extended with ΔP/E — true by construction) plus a **cyclical overlay** (the repricing term captures discount-rate/sentiment shifts). Supply-side content (Ibbotson-Chen; Arnott-Bernstein): incumbent shareholders capture only part of aggregate growth because much accrues to *new* enterprises — so real per-share growth < GDP growth.
- **(Q1) Exists?** Yes, strongly for the identity. Fama-French (2002) apply Gordon to 1951–2000 and find the fundamentals-implied premium (2.55%/4.32%) far below the realized average (7.43%), attributing the gap to a one-time discount-rate decline. Ibbotson-Chen (2003): P/E expansion is a *small* slice of 1926–2000 return.
- **(Q2) Survives being known?** Yes — its strongest feature is that it is **not an alpha signal**, so nothing to arbitrage. The income + growth core is a structural identity plus a paid residual-claim risk premium. Critically, the framework's own logic says the **repricing term does NOT survive** — a P/E cannot rise forever. It survives *because* it flags which component is non-durable.
- **(Q3) Current capture.** Yes as a valuation/expected-return *tool* (no trade to crowd), with two refinements the literature demands: use **total payout not dividends alone** (Straehl-Ibbotson 2017 — dividend-only DDM biases expected return low in the buyback era), and **never plug GDP growth in for g** (the dilution wedge). The "cost" is input estimation error, not transaction cost.
- **(4) Classification (per component).**
  - Dividend/total-payout yield → **PERSISTENT force**
  - Real earnings growth → **PERSISTENT but modest** (< GDP)
  - Inflation pass-through → **PERSISTENT but debated** (softest input)
  - Net buyback/dilution → **CONDITION-DEPENDENT** (regime-shifted)
  - Δvaluation (repricing) → **CONDITION-DEPENDENT — the windfall**
- **(5) 20–30yr evidence.** WILL continue: income yield + real growth (2 centuries Arnott-Bernstein; 125yr DMS). WILL NOT continue: Δvaluation — Grinold-Kroner themselves plugged a *negative* repricing term rather than extrapolate. Assuming real growth = GDP overstates returns by ~1.5–2pp.
- **(6) OOS / international.** Unusually clean. DMS decompose long-run real returns across ~35 countries (1900–2024) and confirm the *structure* — worldwide dominance of dividend income, low real growth, minor/non-uniform multiple change. Straehl-Ibbotson is post-publication *refinement* (total payouts), not refutation.
- **(7) Falsifiers.** Core: a sustained multi-decade multi-country regime where real payout growth systematically exceeds GDP (never observed). "Repricing is durable" is self-falsifying (implies unbounded price/cashflow divergence).
- **(8) Verdict.** **Agree strongly** on income + real-growth core and on repricing being one-time/mean-reverting (best-defended claim). *Weak/contested:* inflation pass-through is the softest input; Straehl-Ibbotson's specific claim that CATY *beats* CAPE drew a formal FAJ 2018 Comment (Arnott-Bernstein) — total-payout direction sound, predictive-superiority magnitude contested; the exact "2%" dilution figure is era/country-dependent.
- **Expression level.** Dividend yield + real growth = **market-wide structural** (belong in a static core). Δvaluation = **market/cyclical** (also style/sector re-ratings; do not budget as a wealth source). Buyback/dilution = **firm-level**, aggregating up — the one component where a cross-sectional tilt could add to the identity rather than merely re-rate it.
- **(9) Citations.** Grinold & Kroner (2002) "The Equity Risk Premium," BGI Investment Insights · Gordon (1959) *Rev. Econ. Stat.* 41(2) · Fama & French (2002) "The Equity Premium," *JF* 57(2) · Ibbotson & Chen (2003) "Long-Run Stock Returns: Participating in the Real Economy," *FAJ* 59(1) · Ilmanen (2011) *Expected Returns*, Wiley.

## 7. Economic Growth ≠ Equity Returns (and Share Dilution)

- **(1) Core claim.** Real per-share equity returns systematically lag aggregate GDP/earnings growth — ~2pp/yr historically — because growth accrues largely to entities *other than incumbent listed shareholders* (new firms, IPOs, private capital, workers, consumers), and existing holders are diluted by net new share issuance; cross-country, faster-growing economies have delivered *lower* real equity returns.
- **(2) Mechanism.** (A) **Dilution** — aggregate earnings grow ~with the economy, but *per-share* growth is lower because share count rises; the metric is net new issuance (offerings + IPOs − buybacks), historically positive. (B) **"Who captures growth"** (Ritter) — growth comes from new not-yet-public firms, from labor/new-capital *inputs*, and from tech progress competed away to *consumers*, not incumbent profits.
- **(Q1) Exists?** Yes, on two independent lines. The ~2% dilution (Bernstein-Arnott 2003). Cross-country: Ritter (2005) finds negative real-return/GDP-growth correlation 1900–2002; Ritter (2012) updates to **−0.39 developed (1900–2011), −0.41 EM (1988–2011)**. DMS corroborate across 44+ countries.
- **(Q2) Survives being known?** Yes — the archetype the mechanism gate privileges. Dilution is a **structural accounting identity** (per-share = aggregate ÷ shares, and shares grow); no incumbent can opt out of being diluted when a rival IPOs. "Growth accrues to consumers/new firms" is competitive equilibrium, not mispricing. Caveat: this validates the *mechanism*; it does NOT make "buy low-growth countries" a live tradeable edge.
- **(Q3) Current capture.** The perverse (negative-to-zero) growth→return relationship is robust — buying high-GDP-growth markets has NOT worked (durable, replicated). But the *inverse* rule ("buy weak-growth countries") is weak — a valuation/mean-reversion story driven by a few episodes, not a clean tradeable factor (Seiler et al. 2024 qualify it as regime/horizon-dependent).
- **(4) Classification.** **PERSISTENT force** for the dilution mechanism and the absence of a positive growth-return link (identity + competitive equilibrium). **CONDITION-DEPENDENT** for the *magnitude* ("2%", "−0.39") and any inverted trading rule.
- **(5) 20–30yr evidence.** Will continue: economies always grow partly through new firms/new equity, so incumbent per-share growth mechanically lags. Magnitude may drift: US net issuance turned sharply negative in the buyback era, mechanically *compressing* per-share dilution for the US specifically — sign persists, quantitative drag can shrink.
- **(6) OOS / international.** Strong; the finding was *born* out-of-sample (multi-country, century-long). Ritter (2012) extended to 2011 and it held; DMS re-confirm through 2024; Seiler et al. (2024) independently replicate. One of the best-surviving empirical regularities in the return literature.
- **(7) Falsifiers.** Net-issuance sign flip: sustained negative net issuance + per-share growth outpacing GDP would break the drag for that market (the post-2010 US buyback era is the live test). Cross-country: a robust replicated *positive* GDP-growth→return correlation in fresh panels. Caveat: private/pre-IPO value capture (tech staying private longer) can *widen* the wedge even as public buybacks shrink the issuance component — the two partly offset, which is why magnitude is unstable while direction is durable.
- **(8) Verdict.** **Agree** — one of the better-grounded structural claims. High confidence on the dilution mechanism and the absence of a positive growth-return link. *Weak/contested:* precise magnitude is sample-dependent; the inverted "buy-low-growth" rule is not a robust edge; the US buyback era compresses aggregate dilution, so don't apply "2%" uncritically to a US-heavy book.
- **Expression level.** Primary expression is **market-wide / country-level** (Ritter, DMS are country panels — cleanest, best-evidenced). Firm-level cousin: **net share issuance** as a cross-sectional characteristic (Pontiff-Woodgate 2008 issuance factor — issuers underperform, repurchasers outperform). Style/sector: indirect (glamour sectors/countries over-issue).
- **(9) Citations.** Arnott & Bernstein (2002) "What Risk Premium Is Normal?" *FAJ* 58(2) · Bernstein & Arnott (2003) "Earnings Growth: The Two Percent Dilution," *FAJ* 59(5) · Ritter (2005) "Economic Growth and Equity Returns," *Pacific-Basin Finance J.* 13(5) · Ritter (2012) "Is Economic Growth Good for Investors?" *J. Applied Corp. Finance* 24(3) · Pontiff & Woodgate (2008) "Share Issuance and Cross-Sectional Returns," *JF* 63(2).

## 8. Capital Allocation, Buybacks & Total Payout

*Two claims held strictly separate.*

- **(1) Core claim.** (A) Total payout (dividends + net buybacks) is the economically correct measure of shareholder cash flow, so a better return anchor/predictor than dividend yield alone. (B) — *separate & much weaker* — firm-level capital-allocation *discipline* (buying back cheap stock, investing only above cost of capital) creates durable per-share alpha.
- **(2) Mechanism.** Under Miller-Modigliani (1961), payout *form* is irrelevant in frictionless markets, so the meaningful cash flow is all cash returned net of cash raised. Post-1982 (SEC Rule 10b-18) firms substituted buybacks for dividends at scale, so dividend yield became a mechanically downward-biased proxy — the measurement mechanism (A). Discipline (B) is a Jensen (1986) free-cash-flow *friction* story: managers with excess cash over-invest/empire-build, so returning cash is value-protective.
- **(Q1) Exists?** Yes for A. Substitution is real (Grullon-Michaely 2002); the dividend proxy broke (Fama-French 2001: payers 66.5%→20.8%, 1978–1999); payout yield predicts returns better (Boudoukh et al. 2007); total payout anchors long-run returns 1871–2014 (Straehl-Ibbotson 2017). **Contested for B:** buyback *timing* is mixed — some firms buy ~2% cheaper than average, but many buy back *overvalued*, and less-constrained/well-connected CEOs buy at *higher* prices.
- **(Q2) Survives being known?** A survives **by construction** — a measurement identity, not a trade. B needs a durable friction: Jensen agency cost is structural and slow-moving (a slice can survive), but any component that pays as a *learnable signal* is arbitrageable. Honest reading: B is partly durable friction, partly arbitrageable signal.
- **(Q3) Current capture.** Time-series return predictability from payout yield is **fragile OOS** (Goyal-Welch 2008) — use total payout to *measure/anchor*, not to *time*. Cross-sectional shareholder-yield strategies are live but hurt by high turnover (lumpy buybacks) — a value/quality cousin, not a free lunch. The quality angle survives better: QMJ (Asness-Frazzini-Pedersen 2019) includes payout and works across 24 countries — but notes the market does *not* obviously pay up for payout.
- **(4) Classification.** A: **PERSISTENT force** (accounting identity, strengthens as buybacks grow). B: **CONDITION-DEPENDENT / partial durable friction** — the agency-cost core persists; the tradeable alpha is regime- and crowding-sensitive (governance regimes, 2023 US 1% buyback excise tax, valuation levels).
- **(5) 20–30yr evidence.** A continues as long as buybacks are material (entrenched since 1982). B won't reliably continue *as alpha*: the market-wide buyback story is **capped by dilution** — what matters is *net* buybacks, and 20th-century per-share growth ran ~2% below GDP; the aggregate "buybacks juice returns" narrative is largely illusion once dilution is netted.
- **(6) OOS / international.** Predictability decays OOS (Goyal-Welch — strongest negative). QMJ replicates across 24 countries (best support for payout-as-quality). Straehl-Ibbotson was formally **contested** — Arnott & Bernstein Comment (*FAJ* 74(1), 2018) disputing the buyback/dilution accounting; a live dispute, not settled.
- **(7) Falsifiers.** For B: if the modal firm buys back pro-cyclically at peaks, discipline is not a population trait (evidence shows firms do buy high under weak governance). For the strong market-wide claim: dilution swamps gross buybacks — always use *net*. Tax/regulatory shifts (2023 excise tax) alter the mix.
- **(8) Verdict.** Claim A (total payout is the better measure/anchor): **Agree, strong** — near an accounting truth in the buyback era; its return-*forecasting* extension is weak OOS and was formally contested — measure, don't time. Claim B (discipline → durable alpha): **Genuinely weak / contested** — Jensen mechanism is real and slow-moving, QMJ gives some support, but the market-wide version dies once dilution is netted, firms demonstrably buy back overvalued under weak governance, and any tradeable signal decays. Best understood as one input to a quality tilt with modest, regime-dependent, decaying edge — not standalone durable alpha.
- **Expression level.** Total payout is a **market-wide return-accounting** component (net of dilution ≈ dividends + net buybacks tracking the real economy). Discipline and buyback/dilution express **at the firm level** — the cross-sectional spread between per-share compounders and dilutive empire-builders is where any real signal lives, intrinsically a *quality-factor characteristic*, not a market-timing lever. The market aggregate mostly washes out (dilution ≈ gross buybacks); firm-level dispersion is where the economics are real but harvestable alpha is contested.
- **(9) Citations.** Miller & Modigliani (1961) "Dividend Policy, Growth, and the Valuation of Shares," *J. Business* 34(4) · Fama & French (2001) "Disappearing Dividends," *JFE* 60 · Boudoukh, Michaely, Richardson & Roberts (2007) "On the Importance of Measuring Payout Yield," *JF* 62 · Straehl & Ibbotson (2017) "The Long-Run Drivers of Stock Returns," *FAJ* 73(3) (+ Arnott-Bernstein Comment, *FAJ* 74(1), 2018) · Jensen (1986) "Agency Costs of Free Cash Flow," *AER* 76(2).

## 9. Dividend Reinvestment & the Arithmetic of Compounding

**Granularity flag up front.** This is a **market-wide / total-return-accounting** force at the *index* level — NOT a firm-selection edge. "Reinvested dividends dominate long-run total return" is about the index total-return series vs the price series; it says nothing about picking high-dividend stocks. Conflating the two is the most common error here.

- **(1) Core claim.** Over multi-decade horizons the overwhelming majority of an equity index's real *total*-return accumulation comes from dividends **reinvested and left to compound**, not price appreciation — the total-return series diverges enormously from the price-only series.
- **(2) Mechanism.** Two bundled things kept separate: (a) **accounting decomposition** — the dividend-yield term has historically been the largest piece because real per-share growth was low (Arnott: ~0.8%/yr real over 200yr, ≈0 since 1965); (b) **compounding arithmetic** — reinvested dividends buy shares that themselves pay dividends; total-return index = ∏(1+p+d) vs price index ∏(1+p), the gap growing super-linearly in horizon. A multiplier, **not alpha** — what you mechanically get by not consuming the cash.
- **(Q1) Exists?** Yes, strongly. Arnott (2003) "three dwarfs": of ~7.9% nominal over 200yr, ~5.0% was dividends vs 1.4% inflation / 0.6% valuation / 0.8% real growth. DMS (2002): $1 → **$16,797 with dividends reinvested vs $198 price-only** over 1900–2000 (~85×). Fisher-Lorie (1964): NYSE 1926–1960 = 9.0%/yr compounded, tax-exempt, reinvested. Siegel: ~97% of *real* accumulation over ~200yr from reinvested dividends.
- **(Q2) Survives being known?** Trivially yes — it is **arithmetic, not an edge**. Nothing to arbitrage; survives being known like ∏(1+r) does. But that same triviality means it confers **no advantage over any other reinvesting index holder** — it is not relative outperformance.
- **(Q3) Current capture (costs/taxes/buyback shift).** Dividend taxes: a taxable investor reinvests post-tax, a genuine drag (Fisher-Lorie/Siegel headline figures are tax-exempt). The buyback shift attacks the *"dividends"* framing, not the compounding: framed as *dividend yield*, dominance shrinks in the buyback era; framed as *total payout / total return*, the compounding force is fully intact (buybacks return cash via price, already in the total-return index).
- **(4) Classification.** Compounding arithmetic (b): **PERSISTENT / invariant** (identity, cost/tax-modulated in magnitude only). Dominance of *dividends specifically* (a): **CONDITION-DEPENDENT** — depended on historically high payout ratios + low real growth; the dividend-yield share is structurally lower in the buyback era.
- **(5) 20–30yr evidence.** Compounding multiplier: guaranteed by construction if anything is returned and reinvested. Dividend-yield-specific dominance: likely *lower* than the 5%/97% historical figures — payout shifted to buybacks, starting yields low (~1.5–2% vs 4–5% historically), real per-share dividend growth ≈0 since 1965.
- **(6) OOS / international.** Strong and broad — DMS built the canonical OOS dataset (16→35 countries, 1900–present). The reinvested-vs-consumed gap and large dividend share replicate across nearly all developed markets over 120+yr; not a US-only artifact.
- **(7) Falsifiers.** Arithmetic (b): none — an identity; only "disappearance" is behavioral (consuming dividends) or frictional (taxes eat the reinvested base). Dominance-of-*dividends* (a): a regime of near-zero dividend payout with cash returned entirely via buybacks — **partially happening already**. *Data-integrity flag:* Edward McQuarrie disputes Siegel's pre-1926 record (early dividends often consumed/taxed/imperfectly reinvested), softening the clean "97%" headline for the deep-historical period.
- **(8) Verdict.** Compounding arithmetic: **Agree without reservation — bulletproof**, but precisely *because* it is arithmetic it is **not an edge**; the operational lesson is hold total-return exposure and reinvest. "Dividends specifically dominate future returns": **era-sensitive — partial disagree** (restate as *total shareholder payout* and it holds; keep it as *dividend yield* and it decays). "High-dividend *stocks* outperform": **separate, contested value/quality factor claim — do not conflate.**
- **Expression level.** **Market-wide / total-return-accounting** force at the aggregate/index level. A reason to reinvest and stay invested at the *index* level — never evidence for a dividend-stock selection sleeve.
- **(9) Citations.** Fisher & Lorie (1964) "Rates of Return on Investments in Common Stocks," *J. Business* 37(1) · DMS (2002) *Triumph of the Optimists*, Princeton UP · Arnott (2003) "Dividends and the Three Dwarfs," *FAJ* 59(2) · Fama & French (2001) "Disappearing Dividends," *JFE* 60(1) · Boudoukh et al. (2007) *JF* 62(2); Siegel, reassessed in CFA Research Foundation (2026) *Stocks for the Long Run Revisited* (McQuarrie critique).

# PART III — VALUATION, PREDICTABILITY & THE PRICE OF REPRICING

## 10. Shiller CAPE / Campbell-Shiller Long-Horizon Predictability

- **(1) Core claim.** High valuation ratios (low D/P, high CAPE) forecast low subsequent long-horizon real returns; almost all variation in price-dividend ratios reflects changing *expected returns*, not changing expected dividend growth.
- **(2) Mechanism.** The Campbell-Shiller (1988) log-linear present-value identity: a high price-dividend ratio must be followed *either* by high future dividend growth *or* by low future returns. Empirically dividend growth is nearly unforecastable, so the ratio must move because discount rates move. Economically, discount rates rise in bad times (higher risk aversion / worse prospects), depressing prices and setting up high forward returns — a time-varying risk premium.
- **(Q1) Exists?** Yes, robustly in-sample and at long horizons. The identity is not disputable; the loading of price variation onto expected-return (vs cash-flow) variation is the strong finding of Campbell-Shiller (1988) and the organizing fact of Cochrane's (2011) "Discount Rates."
- **(Q2) Survives being known?** This is a *paid risk / structural* channel, not a free lunch. Publicizing "buy when CAPE is low" does not arbitrage it away because low-CAPE states are genuinely bad states to bear risk in — the premium is compensation, not mispricing. The strongest reason the *phenomenon* is durable.
- **(Q3) Current capture.** This is where it weakens sharply. As a *timing tool* the OOS record is poor: Goyal-Welch (2008) show valuation-ratio predictor regressions largely fail OOS vs a simple historical mean, are unstable, and would not have helped in real time. CAPE timing suffers hindsight bias (Asness-Ilmanen-Maloney 2017 — you cannot know the future CAPE distribution in real time) and long "wrong" stretches. As a *long-horizon expected-return input* (setting 10-yr assumptions, not trading) it remains defensible.
- **(4) Classification.** **PERSISTENT economic force** (time-varying risk premium) at its core — but the tradable *timing signal* is closer to a **CONDITION-DEPENDENT / fragile pattern.**
- **(5) 20–30yr evidence.** The identity and risk-premium logic will continue. The *magnitude and stability* of the CAPE→return mapping likely will not: structural shifts (lower rates, higher intangible share understating E, buyback-shifted payout) plausibly raise the "fair" CAPE, so mechanical reversion to a 1926 mean is suspect.
- **(6) OOS / international.** Weak and mixed. Goyal-Welch (2008) is the canonical OOS-failure result; Campbell-Thompson (2008) restore *modest* OOS predictability only by imposing sign/steady-state constraints; Rapach-Strauss-Zhou (2010) recover gains via forecast combination. Internationally weaker/unstable. Predictability survives publication only in a heavily disciplined, constrained form.
- **(7) Falsifiers.** Falsified as a *timing edge* if constrained OOS R² is indistinguishable from zero in fresh data/countries (largely the Goyal-Welch verdict). Falsified as *economics* only if price-dividend variation started being matched by realized dividend-growth variation (cash-flow, not discount-rate, driven).
- **(8) Verdict.** **Agree** on the economics (discount-rate variation is real and paid). **Genuinely weak/contested** as an exploitable timing signal after costs and OOS — the constrained versions barely clear the historical-mean bar. Directly relevant to this project's closed market-timing nulls: this is the literature that already says the timing version loses.
- **Expression level.** **Market-wide** (also applies to any risky asset via its own discount rate). The durable object is the risk premium, not any timing signal.
- **(9) Citations.** Campbell & Shiller (1988) "Stock Prices, Earnings, and Expected Dividends," *JF* 43(3) · Cochrane (2011) "Presidential Address: Discount Rates," *JF* 66 · Goyal & Welch (2008) "A Comprehensive Look…," *RFS* 21(4) · Campbell & Thompson (2008) "Predicting Excess Stock Returns Out of Sample," *RFS* 21(4) · Asness, Ilmanen & Maloney (2017) "Market Timing: Sin a Little," *JPM*/AQR.

## 11. The Predictability Debate (Goyal-Welch vs Campbell-Thompson vs Cochrane)

- **(1) Core claim.** Whether the equity premium is *forecastable in real time* is genuinely contested: strong in-sample evidence, weak-but-nonzero OOS evidence once constrained, and a theoretical consensus (Cochrane) that expected returns *must* vary even if we forecast them poorly.
- **(2) Mechanism.** Same discount-rate logic as School 10. The debate is econometric: in-sample fit vs OOS honesty, parameter instability, look-ahead in predictor choice, and the low signal-to-noise of a volatile annual return series.
- **(Q1) Exists?** The disagreement is real and unresolved. Goyal-Welch (2008): models "predicted poorly both IS and OOS for 30 years." Campbell-Thompson (2008): constraints (non-negative premium, sensible signs) turn OOS R² positive. Both are correct about their own tests — the result is fragile to method.
- **(Q2) Survives being known?** The *theoretical* claim (expected returns vary) survives trivially — an equilibrium necessity (Cochrane). The *empirical edge* survives publication only weakly and only under discipline; unconstrained, it has effectively been arbitraged/overfit away.
- **(Q3) Current capture.** Marginal. Campbell-Thompson's economically-meaningful gain is small and requires prior structure; costs and estimation error erode it. This is the empirical backbone of the Asness (2016) "market timing is a trap; risk management is the edge" position this project already adopted.
- **(4) Classification.** Mixed: **PERSISTENT force** (expected-return variation) wrapped in a **CONDITION-DEPENDENT, fragile empirical signal.**
- **(5–6) Continuation / OOS.** The methodological lesson persists: assume any single-series equity-premium predictor is near-zero OOS until proven under Campbell-Thompson-style constraints and forecast combination, on fresh/international data. Goyal-Welch-Zafirov (2022, "…II") re-examine ~30 predictors and largely reaffirm the fragility with newer data.
- **(7) Falsifiers.** A predictor is "real" only if constrained OOS gains replicate out-of-sample, out-of-country, and post-publication. Most do not.
- **(8) Verdict.** **Agree** with the Goyal-Welch/Asness skeptical baseline as the null; the burden of proof sits on any timing claim. Cochrane's point (returns *do* vary) is correct but is a statement about the world, not a tradable edge.
- **Expression level.** **Market-wide** (equity-premium forecasting). Not sector/firm.
- **(9) Citations.** Welch & Goyal (2008) *RFS* 21(4); Goyal, Welch & Zafirov (2022) "…II," SSRN 3929119 · Campbell & Thompson (2008) *RFS* 21(4) · Rapach, Strauss & Zhou (2010) "Out-of-Sample Equity Premium Prediction," *RFS* 23(2) · Cochrane (2011) *JF* 66.

## 12. Is Valuation EXPANSION a Durable Source or a One-Time Repricing?

- **(1) Core claim.** Rising valuation multiples (P/E expansion) are a *non-recurring, mean-reverting* contributor to realized returns, not a durable compounding engine; durable long-run return comes from dividend yield + real earnings/payout growth.
- **(2) Mechanism.** Bogle's identity: total return ≈ dividend yield + earnings growth ± ΔP/E. The ΔP/E term is bounded — multiples cannot expand forever (that would imply an ever-falling discount rate → zero). Over long horizons the multiple term shrinks toward zero and the fundamental (yield + growth) term dominates. Straehl-Ibbotson (2017) formalize the durable engine as total payout growing with the real economy.
- **(Q1) Exists?** Yes, well-documented mechanically. Decompositions (Bogle; Straehl-Ibbotson 2017; Blitz 2025) consistently show multiple expansion is a minority contributor over multi-decade windows and mean-reverts, while dividends + earnings growth carry the long run.
- **(Q2) Survives being known?** Not applicable as an *edge* — this is a decompositional truth, not a strategy. Its durability is definitional: multiple expansion is self-limiting by arithmetic. Knowing it does not change it.
- **(Q3) Current capture.** You do not "capture" multiple expansion durably — that is the point. The durable capture is owning the payout-growth stream at a non-extreme entry multiple.
- **(4) Classification.** The multiple-expansion component is a **CONDITION-DEPENDENT historical pattern** (regime of falling rates / re-rating); the yield+growth component is the **PERSISTENT force.**
- **(5) 20–30yr evidence.** Yield + real payout growth continues as long as the corporate sector earns and distributes. Multiple expansion of the 1980s–2020s magnitude (driven by a secular rate decline from ~15% to ~0%) is a spent, non-repeatable tailwind — a critical caution for any 20–30yr projection that extrapolates recent total returns.
- **(6) OOS / international.** The decomposition holds across countries; the durable term everywhere is fundamentals, and high-multiple entry points internationally predict weak forward returns (ties back to CAPE).
- **(7) Falsifiers.** Falsified if multiples showed a permanent upward drift unexplained by a permanent discount-rate fall (no evidence) — instead they mean-revert.
- **(8) Verdict.** **Strongly agree.** Treat multiple expansion as a non-recurring windfall, not a return source; anchor long-run assumptions on payout yield + real growth at a sane entry valuation.
- **Expression level.** **Market/cyclical** (also style- and sector-level re-ratings). A timing object, not a wealth source. The paired yield+growth engine is **market-wide structural.**
- **(9) Citations.** Bogle (1999) *Common Sense on Mutual Funds*; Bogle & Nolan (2015) *JPM* · Straehl & Ibbotson (2017) "The Long-Run Drivers of Stock Returns," *FAJ* 73(3) · Ilmanen (2011) *Expected Returns*, Wiley · Blitz (2025) "Decomposing Equity Returns," SSRN 5159811.

# PART IV — INNOVATION, PRODUCTIVITY & INTANGIBLES

*This is where the sector-vs-mechanism discipline matters most.* **Headline: productivity/innovation is the ultimate source of real corporate growth, but the fraction that accrues to *shareholders* is small and contested, and the durable object is an intangible-capital / scalability *mechanism*, not "the tech sector."**

## 13. Innovation, Productivity & Intangibles as a Return Source

- **(1) Core claim.** Long-run real equity growth is ultimately driven by productivity/technological progress; the modern *expression* has shifted from tangible to intangible capital (R&D, software, brand, organizational capital), which is scalable and non-rival and concentrates rents in a few intangible-intensive leaders — but most of the social surplus from innovation flows to *consumers*, not shareholders.
- **(2) Mechanism.**
  - *Growth foundation:* Romer (1990) — ideas are **non-rival** and partially excludable, generating increasing returns; growth is endogenous, produced by profit-seeking R&D. This is why innovation *can* create value.
  - *Modern expression:* Corrado-Hulten-Sichel — intangible investment now rivals tangible investment; because intangibles are scalable and hard to replicate, they support market share and margins. Crouzet-Eberly (2018) — the rise of intangibles is driven by *industry leaders* and coincides with rising concentration, via two distinct channels: **productivity** (strongest in Consumer) and **market power** (strongest in Healthcare).
  - *Who gets paid:* Nordhaus (2004) — producers capture only ~2.2% of the total surplus from their innovations; ~97.8% is competed away to consumers. Kogan-Papanikolaou-Seru-Stoffman (2017) — the market *does* price patent value, but innovation also drives **creative destruction** that reallocates value *away from incumbents* toward new entrants.
- **(Q1) Exists?** Productivity → real growth: yes (Solow residual; Romer endogenous growth). Intangibles as measurable, growing, priced capital: yes — Corrado-Hulten-Sichel (national accounts); Peters-Taylor (2017) show including intangibles *strengthens* the firm-level investment-q relation. Innovation priced in equities: yes — KPSS (2017). **But** shareholder capture is small (Nordhaus 2.2%) and incumbent capture is undermined by creative destruction (Bessembinder's extreme skew).
- **(Q2) Survives being known?** Two genuinely durable structural frictions — the strong part of this cluster:
  - **Non-rivalry + scalability** (Romer): an idea/software/brand serves one more customer at near-zero marginal cost — a *structural* property of intangible production, not a mispricing.
  - **Excludability via intangibles → durable rents** (Crouzet-Eberly market-power channel): patents, network effects, switching costs, organizational capital are *barriers to replication* — "moats" that let *some* firms retain a slice of the surplus.
  - What does NOT survive: "buy innovation because it's innovative" — Nordhaus + the growth-vs-returns disconnect say the average innovation dollar is a consumer subsidy, and creative destruction means today's innovator is tomorrow's disrupted incumbent.
- **(Q3) Current capture — contested.** The intangible-capital *value* premium (buying firms whose intangibles are underpriced by GAAP, which expenses R&D/SG&A) has some support (Peters-Taylor) — an accounting-mismeasurement mechanism plausibly persisting while accounting rules lag. But *naively* buying high-innovation / high-growth firms is not reliable: Nordhaus says surplus leaks to consumers; Bessembinder (2018) shows ~4% of firms account for *all* net wealth creation while 57.4% of stocks underperform T-bills — you must hold the right handful, and identifying them ex ante is unproven.
- **(4) Classification.** Productivity/innovation as *source of real growth*: **PERSISTENT economic force.** Non-rivalry/scalability and excludability (intangible moats): **PERSISTENT structural frictions.** **"The technology sector outperforms": CONDITION-DEPENDENT historical pattern / recent-winner artifact** unless derived from the mechanism (see below).
- **(5) 20–30yr evidence.** The intangible-capital *mechanism* will very likely persist and probably intensify (software/AI raise scalability and excludability). NOT guaranteed: (a) that shareholders rather than consumers capture the surplus (Nordhaus is a strong prior against); (b) that the *specific incumbents* who dominate today keep the rents (creative destruction). The safe long-run claim is "own the broad market and you own whatever firms end up capturing the durable intangible rents," not "own the current leaders."
- **(6) OOS / international.** Corrado-Hulten-Sichel intangible-accounting replicates across OECD (INTAN-Invest, EU KLEMS). The **growth-return disconnect is the key OOS check and it is damning for the naive version:** Ritter (2005) and DMS (2002) find the cross-country correlation between real per-capita GDP growth and real equity returns is *negative* over 1900–2002. High-growth economies did *not* deliver high equity returns — because growth is competed away, funded by new issuance (dilution), and capitalized into entry prices. **Aggregate innovation/growth ≠ shareholder returns.**
- **(7) Falsifiers.** Intangible-value edge falsified if accounting standards start capitalizing intangibles (removing the mismeasurement) and the premium vanishes. Sector-innovation "edge" falsified (already largely is) by the growth-return disconnect and rotating sector leadership (railroads → industrials → tech). The durable mechanism would be falsified if intangible-intensive leaders stopped earning persistent excess margins/share (Crouzet-Eberly facts reversing).
- **(8) Verdict.**
  - **Agree (strong evidence):** productivity is the real-growth source; non-rivalry + intangible excludability are durable structural frictions; intangibles are real, priced capital (Romer, Corrado-Hulten-Sichel, Peters-Taylor, Crouzet-Eberly, KPSS converge).
  - **Genuinely weak / contested:** that this reliably accrues to *shareholders* as excess returns. Nordhaus (2.2% capture), Ritter/DMS (negative growth-return correlation), Bessembinder (extreme concentration) collectively say the *average* innovation exposure is a poor return source and the *winning* exposure is nearly impossible to pre-identify.
  - **Disagree** with any framing that treats "the tech sector" itself as the durable mechanism.
- **Expression level & the sector question (critical).** It is the **intangible-capital-efficiency / scalability / excludability mechanism**, which *currently* concentrates in technology and parts of healthcare — but the mechanism→sector link is only *partially* derivable:
  - *Derived, legitimate:* Crouzet-Eberly explicitly locate the *productivity* channel in Consumer and the *market-power* channel in Healthcare — so a mechanism→sector mapping exists, but it is "own firms with durable intangible excludability," which spans sectors and shifts over time — NOT "own tech."
  - *Recent-winner artifact to flag:* "Technology has outperformed, therefore technology is the innovation return source" is exactly the inference the discipline forbids. The growth-return disconnect and creative destruction show sector leadership is *not* a stable mechanism expression — it rotates, and the aggregate sector's growth is largely competed away or pre-priced. Today's dominant-innovator sector is a snapshot of *where* the mechanism currently concentrates, not the mechanism itself.
  - *Do innovation profits accrue to shareholders?* Weight of evidence: **mostly competed away / to consumers.** Shareholders capture innovation returns only (a) in the minority of firms with durable excludability that resist competition, and (b) when accounting mismeasurement leaves intangibles underpriced — and identifying those firms ex ante is unproven.
- **(9) Citations.** Romer (1990) "Endogenous Technological Change," *JPE* 98(5) · Corrado, Hulten & Sichel (2009) "Intangible Capital and U.S. Economic Growth," *Rev. Income & Wealth* 55(3) · Crouzet & Eberly (2018) "Understanding Weak Capital Investment: Market Concentration and Intangibles," Jackson Hole / NBER w25869 · Nordhaus (2004) "Schumpeterian Profits in the American Economy," NBER w10433 · Kogan, Papanikolaou, Seru & Stoffman (2017) "Technological Innovation, Resource Allocation, and Growth," *QJE* 132(2) · Peters & Taylor (2017) "Intangible Capital and the Investment-q Relation," *JFE* 123(2) · Bessembinder (2018) "Do Stocks Outperform Treasury Bills?" *JFE* 129(3).

# PART V — COMPOUNDING MECHANICS & THE HORIZON DEBATE

**The clean split.** Part V-A (Kelly / variance drain) is largely **mathematical IDENTITY — durable, survives being known because you cannot arbitrage arithmetic.** Part V-B (time diversification via mean reversion) is **EMPIRICAL and genuinely contested** — the strongest modern paper (Pastor-Stambaugh 2012) *reverses* the reassuring claim.

## 14. Kelly Criterion — Log-Optimal = Long-Run Geometric Growth

- **(1) Core claim.** The allocation fraction that maximizes the *expected logarithm* of wealth each period almost surely maximizes the long-run geometric growth rate and, asymptotically, dominates any essentially-different strategy.
- **(2) Mechanism.** Terminal wealth after N periods is a *product* of gross returns; its log is a *sum* of per-period log-returns, so by the strong law of large numbers the realized growth rate converges to the *expected* log-return. Maximizing E[log(1+f·X)] maximizes the exponential growth rate g. A mathematical fact about multiplicative processes, not an empirical regularity.
- **(Q1) Exists?** Yes — provably (Kelly 1956; Breiman 1961 gave the rigorous asymptotic-dominance proof; Thorp applied it). Not in dispute.
- **(Q2) Survives being known?** It is a **theorem/identity**, not an inefficiency. Knowing it changes nothing — it is the *definition* of what compounding does to a repeated multiplicative game. Fully durable. (What CAN degrade is the *edge* you feed in — not the sizing principle.)
- **(Q3) Current capture.** The *principle* is untouched by crowding (not an alpha source). The catch is entirely on the *input* side: Kelly sizing requires the true return distribution; with estimated parameters, plugging in point estimates causes **overbetting** past the growth-maximizing point (MacLean-Ziemba-Blazenko 1992) — hence **fractional Kelly** (half-Kelly) in practice. Durable principle, real-world capture needs a haircut for parameter uncertainty and costs.
- **(4) Classification.** **PERSISTENT FORCE — mathematical identity.** Same durability tier as compound interest.
- **(5) 20–30yr evidence.** Invariant to era, asset class, or market structure.
- **(6) OOS / international.** "OOS survival" is the wrong frame (not empirical); the operational record (Thorp's Princeton-Newport; Kelly sizing across gambling and markets since the 1960s) confirms practical value across domains.
- **(7) Falsifiers.** Cannot "disappear" as math. Fails as an *investment rule* where assumptions break: single-shot decisions (no LLN), finite horizons where a more-risk-averse investor's utility differs from log, fat-tail/ruin thresholds where a bad draw is terminal before the asymptotics arrive.
- **(8) Verdict.** **Agree — it is an IDENTITY, treat as durable.** The one honest caveat is normative, not factual: log-optimality is only *your* optimum if your utility is log (School 16).
- **Expression level.** **Portfolio / position-sizing & leverage.** Answers "how big," not "which name." Not sector-level.
- **(9) Citations.** Kelly (1956) "A New Interpretation of Information Rate," *Bell System Technical J.* 35 · Breiman (1961) "Optimal Gambling Systems for Favorable Games," *Proc. 4th Berkeley Symp.* · Thorp (2006) "The Kelly Criterion in Blackjack, Sports Betting, and the Stock Market," in MacLean-Thorp-Ziemba (2011) · MacLean, Ziemba & Blazenko (1992) "Growth versus Security in Dynamic Investment Analysis," *Management Science* 38(11).

## 15. Volatility Drag / Variance Drain (g ≈ μ − σ²/2) and the Overbetting Ceiling

- **(1) Core claim.** Compound (geometric) growth is strictly *below* the arithmetic mean by ≈ half the variance — g ≈ μ − σ²/2 — so volatility mechanically erodes long-run wealth, and betting *more* than Kelly raises variance faster than mean and **lowers** geometric growth (eventually to ruin).
- **(2) Mechanism.** For log-returns, E[log(1+R)] ≈ E[R] − ½·Var(R) (second-order Taylor; exact for continuous-time GBM: g = μ − σ²/2). Because growth is quadratic-concave in bet size f, there is an interior maximum (Kelly f*); to the right of it added σ² outruns added μ, so g(f) turns down and crosses zero — past that point wealth → 0 almost surely.
- **(Q1) Exists?** Yes — algebraic identity. Messmore (1995) popularized "variance drain."
- **(Q2) Survives being known?** **Identity — fully durable.** Publicity cannot repeal a Taylor expansion. The overbetting result is a property of the g(f) curve, not a market inefficiency.
- **(Q3) Current capture.** The *implication* — vol-control / avoiding leverage-induced variance improves compounding — is a genuine free lunch in geometric-return space and does not crowd out (not an alpha trade). Leveraged/high-turnover products that ignore drag (daily-rebalanced leveraged ETFs) demonstrate it persistently; costs *reinforce* the case against overbetting.
- **(4) Classification.** **PERSISTENT FORCE — mathematical identity + persistent structural constraint (leverage aversion, funding limits).**
- **(5) 20–30yr evidence.** Invariant.
- **(6) OOS / international.** Universal by construction; the leveraged-ETF decay literature is the recurring live confirmation.
- **(7) Falsifiers.** Only breaks where σ is genuinely zero or the process is not multiplicative. Falsifier: an asset whose realized long-run compound return systematically *exceeds* its arithmetic mean — impossible for positive variance.
- **(8) Verdict.** **Agree — IDENTITY, durable.** The mathematically cleanest "source of long-run wealth" lever: controlling variance is not market-timing alpha, it is a guaranteed geometric-return improvement. **This is the mathematical *why* behind this project's standing vol-targeting / risk-management-is-the-edge thesis.**
- **Expression level.** **Portfolio / whole-book** (variance of total returns and leverage). Sizing and risk-control, not name selection.
- **(9) Citations.** Kelly/Breiman/MacLean-Ziemba-Blazenko trio (as above) · Messmore (1995) "Variance Drain," *JPM* · continuous-time identity g = μ − σ²/2 (Merton 1969).

## 16. Samuelson's Objection — Log-Optimality Is NOT a Normative Rule

- **(1) Core claim.** Maximizing expected log wealth is optimal *only* for an investor whose utility is logarithmic; it is **not** a universal prescription, and the "LLN makes it dominate long-horizon" argument is a fallacy for anyone with different risk aversion.
- **(2) Mechanism.** Expected-utility theory: horizon-optimal allocation depends on utility curvature. Log utility (RRA = 1) gives the Kelly fraction; a more risk-averse investor (RRA > 1) rationally bets *less*, and no horizon lengthening makes the log-optimal bet their optimum — "almost-sure dominance" is in wealth *ranking*, not *expected utility*.
- **(Q1) Exists?** Yes — a correct theorem within EU theory. The Kelly camp concedes it (MacLean-Thorp-Ziemba: log-optimality is *a* criterion, not *the* criterion).
- **(Q2) Survives being known?** Durable — a logical/normative point.
- **(Q3) Current capture.** Very real bite: it is exactly *why* practitioners fractional-Kelly. Essentially a *both-are-right* split — Kelly wins "maximize geometric growth"; Samuelson wins "that objective isn't automatically yours."
- **(4) Classification.** **PERSISTENT — logical/normative fact.**
- **(8) Verdict.** **Agree — a correct and important qualifier, not a refutation.** For a decades-holdable, drawdown-budgeted book, the risk-averse / fractional posture is correct — Samuelson's point operationalized.
- **Expression level.** **Portfolio (utility choice / sizing).**
- **(9) Citations.** Samuelson (1971) "The 'Fallacy' of Maximizing the Geometric Mean…," *PNAS* 68(10) · Samuelson (1979) "Why We Should Not Make Mean Log of Wealth Big Though Years to Act Are Long," *J. Banking & Finance* 3 · MacLean, Thorp & Ziemba (2010/2011) "A Response to Professor Paul A. Samuelson's Objections…," Systemic Risk Centre DP-52.

## 17. Time Diversification — Samuelson's "No" (Horizon Irrelevant Under CRRA)

- **(1) Core claim.** For a CRRA investor facing i.i.d. returns, the optimal equity share is **independent of horizon** — a long horizon does not justify more stocks; "stocks are safer over time" is a fallacy.
- **(2) Mechanism.** Samuelson (1969) / Merton (1969): with CRRA utility and i.i.d. returns, myopic allocation is optimal at every age — horizon terms cancel. The "fallacy of large numbers" (Samuelson 1963): the LLN shrinks the *average* return's variance but *cumulative wealth* variance still grows with horizon; time does not diversify a *cumulative* stake the way cross-sectional diversification splits one stake.
- **(Q1) Exists?** As a theorem under CRRA + i.i.d., airtight.
- **(Q2) Survives being known?** Durable *as a conditional theorem*. Its real-world force hinges entirely on whether returns are actually i.i.d. — the empirical battleground of Schools 18–19.
- **(4) Classification.** **PERSISTENT (as a theorem)** — but its practical relevance is **CONDITION-DEPENDENT on the i.i.d. assumption.**
- **(8) Verdict.** **Agree conditionally.** If returns are i.i.d., Samuelson is exactly right. The whole debate reduces to: are equity returns i.i.d., mean-reverting, or — once you admit parameter uncertainty — effectively mean-*averting*?
- **Expression level.** **Market-wide** (equity share of the book vs horizon). Not sector.
- **(9) Citations.** Samuelson (1963) "Risk and Uncertainty: A Fallacy of Large Numbers," *Scientia* 98 · Samuelson (1969) "Lifetime Portfolio Selection by Dynamic Stochastic Programming," *REStat* 51(3) · Merton (1969) "Lifetime Portfolio Selection under Uncertainty," *REStat* 51(3).

## 18. Time Diversification — Siegel / Fama-French / Poterba-Summers "Yes" (Mean Reversion Makes Stocks Safer)

- **(1) Core claim.** Equity returns contain a *transitory, mean-reverting* component, so annualized return variance falls with horizon and stocks become *less* risky (relative to bonds) over 20+ year holds.
- **(2) Mechanism.** If prices = random walk (permanent) + stationary mean-reverting (transitory) component, long-horizon returns show *negative* autocorrelation: bad runs partly reverse, compressing multi-year variance below the i.i.d. benchmark. Fama-French (1988): large slow-decaying transitory components (25–45% of return variance at 3–5yr, 1926–85). Poterba-Summers (1988): positive short-horizon and negative long-horizon autocorrelation, US + 17 countries. Siegel: no negative 20-year *real* return over ~200yr, 20-yr real stock vol below bonds'.
- **(Q1) Exists? — CONTESTED.** Point estimates say yes, but Fama-French and Poterba-Summers *both* stress the effect is statistically weak — the random-walk null **cannot be rejected** at conventional levels. Suggestive, not decisive.
- **(Q2) Survives being known?** The honest weak point: mean reversion is an **EMPIRICAL REGULARITY, not an identity.** If it reflects irrational over/under-reaction, publicity (since 1988) should attenuate it. If it reflects rational time-varying discount rates, it can persist but is not free "safety" — it is compensation for time-varying risk.
- **(Q3) Current capture.** Weak. Long-horizon mean reversion is nearly untradeable (multi-decade holds, tiny sample of independent long-horizon observations, huge estimation error). "Just hold equities 20 years" has no cost problem but is not an *edge* — it is a bet the historical pattern recurs.
- **(4) Classification.** **CONDITION-DEPENDENT HISTORICAL PATTERN.** Explicitly *not* an identity. Could be a small-sample / specific-era artifact (US survivorship, one long bull, overlapping-data statistics inflating apparent reversion).
- **(5) 20–30yr evidence.** Genuinely uncertain. In-sample US: yes. Siegel's "never a negative 20-yr real return" rests on only ~10 non-overlapping 20-yr blocks.
- **(6) OOS / international.** Mixed-to-weak. Poterba-Summers found it internationally but weakly. Later work (16-country 1870–2020) finds nominal long-run variability *higher* for equities even where real-downside risk is lower — the "safer long-run" claim is sensitive to real-vs-nominal and to country/era.
- **(7) Falsifiers.** Disappears if (a) reversion was a rational-discount-rate phenomenon that shifts, (b) a US-specific/survivorship artifact, or (c) parameter uncertainty swamps it (→ School 19). Falsifier: OOS long horizons showing variance ratios ≥ 1 (which post-publication and international data increasingly show).
- **(8) Verdict.** **Genuinely weak / contested — flag explicitly.** The mean-reversion evidence is real in-sample but statistically fragile, era- and country-sensitive, and does *not* survive the modern parameter-uncertainty treatment. Do NOT treat "time diversification makes stocks safe" as a durable law. This is the empirical claim, and the one to be skeptical of.
- **Expression level.** **Market-wide** (aggregate equity index horizon risk). Not sector/name.
- **(9) Citations.** Fama & French (1988) "Permanent and Temporary Components of Stock Prices," *JPE* 96(2) · Poterba & Summers (1988) "Mean Reversion in Stock Prices," *JFE* 22(1) · Siegel *Stocks for the Long Run* (1st ed. 1994; 6th ed. 2022).

## 19. Time Diversification — Pastor-Stambaugh (2012) Reversal (Stocks Are MORE Volatile in the Long Run)

- **(1) Core claim.** From a *forward-looking investor's* predictive-variance perspective, equities are **more** volatile per year over long (30–50yr) horizons than over one year — parameter/estimation uncertainty and imperfect predictors more than offset mean reversion, reversing the Siegel conclusion.
- **(2) Mechanism.** The relevant object is *predictive* variance (what an investor facing an unknown data-generating process expects), not historical *realized* variance. Pastor-Stambaugh decompose long-horizon predictive variance into ~five components: (i) i.i.d. uncertainty (grows linearly); (ii) mean reversion (*negative* — the only force helping Siegel); (iii) uncertainty about the *current* expected return; (iv) **estimation risk** — parameters unknown even with 200+ years; (v) **predictor imperfection** — the investor's forecasting variables imperfectly capture the true conditional mean, so uncertainty about *future* expected returns compounds with horizon. Components (iii)–(v), especially **predictor imperfection (dominant)**, grow with horizon and swamp mean reversion.
- **(Q1) Exists?** Yes — a rigorous Bayesian predictive-variance result on the same US data Siegel uses. At a 30-year horizon, variance per year rises above the 1-year value once predictor imperfection is included; effect strengthens toward 50 years. *[Precise variance-ratio magnitudes here from the paper's abstract/secondary summaries, not a line-by-line table read.]*
- **(Q2) Survives being known?** **Durable — essentially a statistical/epistemic identity, not a tradeable anomaly.** A rational Bayesian who admits they don't know the true parameters faces *more* long-run uncertainty; publicity cannot remove genuine parameter uncertainty. Belongs closer to the "identity/structural" tier than the contested empirical tier.
- **(4) Classification.** **PERSISTENT — structural epistemic fact** (parameter/model uncertainty is irreducible). It undercuts School 18's condition-dependent pattern with a *durable* argument.
- **(5) 20–30yr evidence.** Yes — the argument is about the unavoidability of estimation error, which does not vanish with more data at the rate needed to rescue long-horizon safety.
- **(6) OOS / post-publication.** The result *is* the post-publication re-examination of Siegel's own long sample; robust to sub-periods (post-1951 data *strengthens* the predictor-imperfection term). Generalizes better than the mean-reversion claim it rebuts.
- **(7) Falsifiers.** Weakens only if investors could *know* the true expected-return process (perfect predictors → Siegel-like low long-horizon variance) or if parameters were constant and precisely estimable. Falsifier: showing predictor imperfection is negligible / a near-perfect return predictor exists — evidence runs the other way.
- **(8) Verdict.** **Agree — the decisive modern word on the debate.** Mean reversion is real but second-order; the first-order fact is that *we don't know the parameters*, and that ignorance makes the long run riskier, not safer. For a decades-holdable, drawdown-budgeted portfolio this is the load-bearing caution — it argues *against* leaning on "stocks are safe if you just wait."
- **Expression level.** **Market-wide** (investor's predictive variance of the equity allocation).
- **(9) Citations.** Pástor & Stambaugh (2012) "Are Stocks Really Less Volatile in the Long Run?" *JF* 67(2) (NBER w14757) · rebuts Siegel (1994); builds on Fama-French (1988), Poterba-Summers (1988).

# BOTTOM LINE — PERSISTENT vs CONDITION-DEPENDENT, AND THE SECTOR QUESTION

**Biggest agreement across the whole map.** The durable, compoundable, market-wide wealth engine is **income yield (measured as total payout, net of dilution) + modest real growth + the reinvestment/compounding multiplier**, all riding on the **market-wide equity risk premium** — a paid, undiversifiable risk that survives being known by construction (Schools 1, 5, 6, 7, 9). These are identities or paid risks, not alpha; nothing to arbitrage.

**Biggest disagreement / genuinely weak evidence.** (i) The *magnitude* of the forward ERP — materially below the realized US ~6% (~3.5% DMS to ~4.2% Damodaran vs 5.5% survey), the single most decision-relevant caveat. (ii) The *structural explanation* of the premium — long-run risk, habit, and rare disasters are largely observationally equivalent, none uniquely identified, each with a serious live critique; do not treat any one as "the" mechanism. (iii) *Mean-reversion time diversification* (School 18) — statistically fragile, era/country-sensitive, and overturned by Pastor-Stambaugh once parameter uncertainty enters. (iv) *Shareholder capture of innovation* (School 13) — mostly competed away to consumers. (v) *Capital-allocation discipline as durable alpha* (School 8B).

**PERSISTENT forces** (tied to constraints likely to remain — identities, paid risks, durable frictions): the equity risk premium; the yield + real-growth + reinvestment-compounding engine; share dilution / growth-accrues-elsewhere; total-payout accounting; variance drain / Kelly geometric-growth mechanics (pure identities — the mathematical *why* behind this project's vol-targeting thesis); Pastor-Stambaugh irreducible parameter uncertainty; and the intangible-capital scalability/excludability *mechanism*.

**CONDITION-DEPENDENT patterns** (may not recur): valuation *expansion* / multiple re-rating (a spent, non-repeatable rate-driven tailwind — do NOT extrapolate recent total returns); CAPE/valuation *timing* as an OOS edge; mean-reversion "stocks are safe long-run"; the specific realized US ~6% premium; and "the technology sector" as an innovation proxy.

**Does any durable mechanism route through a sector today — and why?** Only one candidate does, and only *partially*: the **intangible-capital / scalability / non-rivalry / excludability** mechanism. It is durable and *currently concentrates* in technology and parts of healthcare, and the concentration is partly *derivable* — Crouzet-Eberly locate the productivity channel in Consumer and the market-power channel in Healthcare. **But the durable object is the mechanism (intangible-capital efficiency, moats, scalability), not the sector.** Sector leadership rotates (railroads → industrials → tech), most innovation surplus leaks to consumers (Nordhaus ~2.2% producer capture), the cross-country growth-return correlation is *negative*, and ~4% of firms create all net wealth (Bessembinder). So "own tech because innovation is durable" is a **recent-winner inference the discipline forbids**; the defensible expression is broad market ownership plus, optionally, an intangible-adjusted quality/value tilt with an accounting-mismeasurement rationale — expressed at the **firm / intangible-intensity level**, not as a static sector bet. Every *other* durable mechanism in this map expresses at the **market-wide or portfolio (sizing/leverage) level**, not the sector level.
